# Camera and light vocabulary

These are visual direction cues. Focal length, aperture and color-temperature words in a prompt are not guarantees of actual capture metadata or exposed model controls. Prefer the visual behavior that matters; use numbers when they help communicate that behavior.

## Focal-length family

Ultra-wide (roughly 14–20mm in a full-frame-equivalent vocabulary) suggests expansive space and possible edge distortion. Wide 24–35mm suggests environmental context. Around 50mm suggests a conventional normal-lens view. Portrait/short telephoto around 85–135mm suggests tighter framing and separation. Long telephoto 200mm+ can suggest compressed layers from a distant camera position.

Perspective depends on camera position, not just focal length. Sensor format, distance and lens design affect the result. Do not describe 50mm as literally human vision or promise zero distortion.

## Aperture and depth

Use f/1.2–f/1.8 as a shorthand for very shallow focus when appropriate; f/2–f/2.8 for selective focus; f/4–f/5.6 for broader subject coverage; f/8–f/11 for a deeper-focus intent. Small apertures do not make everything automatically sharp, especially at macro distances.

Useful phrases from the source: razor-thin focus isolating the near eye; soft shallow focus with creamy background dissolve; environment readable but subordinate; deep focus from foreground to horizon; selective focus on one element. Choose a coherent focus plane for multi-person or product detail shots.

## Motion in a still

Frozen action; slight softness at extremities; visible motion blur; imperfect handheld framing; panning blur with a relatively sharp subject and streaked background. These describe one image, not a continuous motion instruction.

## Source families

Natural: direct golden-hour sun; harsh midday light; soft overcast; blue-hour ambient; direct or diffused window light; dappled light through leaves/blinds; bounce from water, walls or ground.

Practical: candle/fire; tungsten lamp; fluorescent; neon signage; screen/TV glow; sodium or LED street light; paper lantern. Match the visible light quality and color to the scene rather than assigning fixed temperatures to all sources of a type.

Studio: softbox, beauty dish, ring light, modified strobe, rim/hair light and reflector fill. Use studio treatments when they fit the requested result; they are not forbidden merely because a source example favored natural light.

Directions: front for even illumination; three-quarter for sculptural modeling; side for split light; back for rim/silhouette; high front for butterfly-like facial shadow; below for upward shadows; three-quarter back for edge separation. Descriptive names do not replace checking source size, position and shadow logic.

## Surface response

Skin: visible texture where the intended medium and scale support it; warm/cool/mixed reflection; plausible moisture and shadow density. Avoid forcing pores or anti-smoothing directives into flat illustration or polished product work.

Fabric/material: weave, drape, compression, translucency, metallic reflection, matte absorption, wetness and specular highlights should agree with the surface and light. Review hands/grip, contact shadows and reflections when visible.

Watch for source/shadow disagreement, hard shadows from a stated large soft source, unjustified rim light, inconsistent reflections or glowing surfaces the brief did not request. Several real light sources may produce several shadows; no-shadow graphic styles can be intentional.
